Before We Solve for X · Continuous Classroom Story

00:00 · Start with a prerequisite

When I teach Algebra One, I use what I call ladder lessons. I start with a skill my students already need, and we use it to reach the next one. Today, that starts with a strip of paper.

00:11 · Compare the two students

I put an equation on the board. X plus three fourths equals one. Before anybody solves it, I ask, “How sure are you that you could do this on your own?”

The boy in the yellow sweatshirt is pretty sure. He subtracts three fourths from both sides and writes one half. The girl beside him is barely sure at all. She writes one fourth, and she can show me why.

If you teach Algebra One, you've probably had a moment like this. Where would you start with him? He already knows what to subtract. Klee-Schramm and colleagues describe this in their review of algebra difficulties. A student can follow a procedure and still be working out what the numbers mean.

00:50 · Model the missing fourth

So I ask him to put three fourths on a line from zero to one. Where would it go? He marks a spot. I ask him to talk me through it.

Booth and colleagues found that how accurately Algebra One students estimated fraction sizes predicted how much they learned in an equation unit. His mark gives me a way into the conversation.

I lay the paper on his desk and fold it into four equal parts. When he opens it up, I run my finger along each space between the creases. Payne and colleagues found explicit teaching and visual models were common in interventions for high school students who struggle with math. That's what I'm doing here. I'm showing him exactly what counts as a fourth.

Now he starts at three fourths. I ask, “How much farther to one?” He touches the last space. One fourth. We put that beside his equation. He keeps the subtraction step and changes his answer. This time he can tell me why.

01:44 · Check what sticks

I want to know whether that explanation will stick. Prosser and Bismarck studied a hands-on square-root lesson in an Algebra One inclusion class. Students scored higher on a later check, even though most felt the model hadn't helped. So I'll ask him how the strip felt today. Tomorrow I'll give him a fresh problem and see what he remembers.

02:03 · Rate confidence before solving

For the next check, I write x plus five eighths equals one. Again, both students rate how sure they are before they write their answers. A one means, “I'm really unsure.” A four means, “I think I can solve this on my own.” That's the idea behind self-efficacy. I'm asking about this particular equation.

02:22 · Keep context and format comparable

Watch what happens if I lay a yardstick on the desk and stretch five eighths of a yard of ribbon along it. The girl can point to the missing length. Would she feel equally sure looking at the symbols alone? In a study of linear functions, Siefer and colleagues found that students judged their ability differently when a problem came with a real-life situation.

Even the way I ask for an answer matters. Pajares and Miller found that middle schoolers reported similar confidence when they chose an answer and when they wrote one. But with written answers, scores were lower and confidence was a poorer guide to performance. That's why I use written answers on both of my checks.

02:59 · Give specific feedback

Now look back at our two students. With him, I want to see whether he can find that missing distance on a new number line. With her, I want to return to something she did right.

I tell her, “You found the missing fourth, and you checked it by adding it back. What could you check the same way with eighths?” Zakariya's review describes how successful practice and specific feedback can build confidence. Now she has a reason to trust her next step.

03:25 · Discuss, then check independently

She shows her partner how she checked. He points to the gap he missed earlier. Granello and colleagues found that working together could strengthen middle schoolers' beliefs about their math ability, while confidence and achievement didn't always change together. So after they've talked, I give each of them a new equation to try alone.

When I look at their work tomorrow, I'll put each student's prediction beside their explanation. Can he show what the fraction means? Can she recognize when her reasoning is sound? Those answers tell me where to start the next ladder lesson.
