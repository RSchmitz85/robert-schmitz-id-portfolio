Audience and problem

Empathy needs a workable next step.

New customer-service employees at a fictional online retailer must handle a delayed order after an earlier unverified reassurance. The customer has a real deadline and asks for compensation beyond the representative’s authority.

The designed performance is an accurate, authorized plan with explicit ownership of follow-up. A friendly response alone is insufficient.

Learning objectives

By the end, the learner should be able to:

  • Acknowledge the customer’s specific situation and distinguish verified facts from assumptions.
  • Offer actions within policy and escalate a larger request without promising approval.
  • Repair an inaccurate promise and confirm who will follow up, how and when.

Role and tools: self-directed instructional design and development; PowerPoint layouts, native Storyline controls and variables, HyperFrames video production, and AI-assisted writing, illustration and build automation. No client or learner-impact results are implied.

My instructional design process

ADDIE, applied to a difficult conversation.

I used the performance goal to connect each phase: help a new representative turn an uncertain delivery into an accurate, authorized plan. Here is how the design decisions became a working lesson.

Analysis

Define the performance gap.

A helpful tone needs dependable follow-through.

For this self-directed concept, I defined a plausible gap: a new representative can sound empathetic yet leave a customer with an unsupported promise. The delayed delivery, earlier reassurance and compensation request create the conditions for that mistake.

The audience and policy are fictional design assumptions. I translated the brief into observable decisions and supplied an order record and service policy so the learner can distinguish evidence from assumptions.

02

Design

Align practice and assessment.

Let the consequences teach the decision.

I designed five decisions around acknowledgment, diagnosis, policy fit, escalation and follow-through. Each offers three plausible responses with a specific customer consequence. Reconnecting branches keep the lesson manageable while saved choices preserve the history of the conversation.

The rubric requires at least 8/10 and a complete, authorized plan. An explicit later correction can repair an open promise; appropriate escalation can earn full credit.

Development

Turn the design into behavior.

Build a conversation that remembers.

I developed editable PowerPoint layouts, then added native Storyline controls, variables, feedback layers and replay behavior. The 38-slide lesson records the learner’s actual responses and uses them in the outcome and personalized debrief.

Poly and Poppins establish the visual hierarchy; illustrated customer scenes and four captioned videos support the conversation. Production review included base slides and selected states, with corrections to the conversation-guide controls.

Evaluation

Check the build; plan the pilot.

Test the rules, then observe the learner.

Completed: a saved-project trigger audit checked all 243 decision combinations. Player checks exercised representative successful, recovery and unsuccessful paths, including a 9/10 attempt that correctly failed for missing a follow-up time. Review and replay were also checked.

Next: complete accessibility and broader browser validation, then pilot with representative new service employees. I would record unsupported promises, missing commitments, resource use and completion time, and revise the confusing decisions before a workplace trial. No learner pilot or measured performance gains have been collected.

Assessment approach

Recover without erasing the mistake.

Five criteria each earn 0–2 points: acknowledgment, diagnosis, policy fit, escalation judgment and follow-through. Success requires at least 8/10, no unresolved unauthorized promise and a complete follow-up commitment.

The final response can explicitly repair an earlier mistake. It clears the unresolved promise, while the original decision still determines its criterion score. Review is read-only; replay starts a new attempt.

Decision structure

  1. AcknowledgeRespond to the specific impact.
  2. InvestigateUse current evidence.
  3. OfferStay within policy.
  4. EscalateKeep ownership.
  5. CommitRepair, then confirm follow-up.

Each decision has three options and tailored feedback. Branches reconnect; saved choices and open promises determine clear plan, credible recovery or work remains.
