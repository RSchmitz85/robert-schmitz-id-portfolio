Professional Development & Adult Learning Design

Lead-teacher mentoring and facilitated PD framed as adult learning design: clear outcomes, active practice, and transfer into day-to-day instructional work.

Role: Facilitator · mentor · lead teacher Audience: Educators (adult learners) Focus: Transfer, not sit-and-get

Overview

Professional development is instructional design for adults. As a lead teacher and mentor who facilitated PD, I treated sessions the way I treat learner modules: analyze the performance gap, write usable objectives, design practice in the room, and plan follow-up so new moves survive contact with Monday morning.

This representative session design shows how I structure adult practice, peer feedback, and a plan for using the skill at work. Participant outcomes for this portfolio artifact have not been measured.

Challenge

One-shot workshops often produce polite notes and little change. Teachers (like other adult professionals) need relevance, respect for prior expertise, time to practice, and a bridge back to their own materials—curriculum maps, assessments, or lesson architectures they will actually use.

The design problem: structure PD so participants leave with rehearsed instructional moves and artifacts they can apply, not only slides they heard.

Audience

  • Secondary educators refining practice in mathematics instruction, assessment, or curriculum use
  • Colleagues receiving mentoring or coaching as part of lead-teacher support
  • Adult learners who expect professional respect and immediate job relevance (ARCS / andragogy)

My role

  • Needs sensing with colleagues (what was breaking in instruction or assessment)
  • Session outcomes, agenda design, and facilitation
  • Mentoring and modeling of instructional practice
  • Job aids / follow-up resources for transfer
  • Connecting PD topics to curriculum and assessment systems in use

Constraints

  • Limited contract time for PD (often a single period or afternoon)
  • Heterogeneous prior knowledge among participants
  • Must respect teachers as experts in their own classrooms
  • Transfer has to work without a large follow-up budget

Design process

  1. Analyze

    Identified the target performance (e.g., using a lesson phase more deliberately; interpreting assessment evidence; implementing a practice structure) and constraints of the PD slot.

  2. Design for adults

    Wrote outcomes in performance language; planned activation of prior practice; reserved the majority of time for guided tryouts rather than lecture.

  3. Facilitate

    Modeled, coached peer practice, and used collaborative analysis of student work or lesson artifacts when available.

  4. Support transfer

    Closed with a concrete next-step commitment and a simple follow-up resource (agenda checklist, planning template, or look-fors).

Design decisions

  • Practice in the room Adults need rehearsal with feedback; PD that is only presentation rarely changes practice.
  • Artifacts over abstractions Worked from real curriculum/assessment materials participants already touch—including systems like REM lesson phases when relevant.
  • Mentoring as personalized ID One-to-one mentor conversations used the same analyze–design–try–reflect loop as formal sessions.
  • ARCS for professional audiences Relevance and confidence first; avoid deficit framing that shuts down experienced teachers.

Solution

A repeatable PD design pattern:

  • Clear performance outcome for the session
  • Short model → collaborative practice → debrief
  • Job-embedded artifacts (planning templates, look-fors, sample student pages)
  • Explicit transfer plan for the next instructional day or PLC

This pattern is portable to corporate L&D and higher-ed faculty development: same adult-learning logic, different content domain.

Tools

Facilitation Mentoring / coaching Session agendas & job aids ARCS · GRR LMS distribution when needed

The session design pattern, documented

Rather than scans of past handouts, the artifacts below document the repeatable session architecture itself — the same pattern applied across topics, shown here carried by a representative 60-minute session on self-checking practice structures.

Session agenda — 60 minutes outcome: leave with one practice structure planned for your own class
  • 0–5
    Frame the gap

    One real student-work sample; what feedback did this learner get, and when?

  • 5–15
    Model

    Facilitator walks one self-checking structure with actual lesson materials — not slides about it.

  • 15–40
    Collaborative practice

    Pairs adapt the structure to a lesson from their own upcoming week; facilitator coaches.

  • 40–50
    Debrief the design

    What did you change and why? Surface adaptation decisions, not compliance.

  • 50–60
    Commit & transfer

    Each participant names the class period and date they will run it; leaves with the look-fors aid.

Transfer job aid look-fors for the first classroom run
  • Students can say how they know an answer is rightSelf-verification is visible, not implied
  • Feedback arrives during practice, not after gradingThe structure — decoder, key, arrival signal — does the checking
  • Work is shown, not just answers recordedThe self-check rewards the route, not the guess
  • Wrong answers produce a next actionLearners know what to re-try, not only that they missed
  • One thing to adjust before the second runIteration is the expectation, not a failure signal

The practice structures referenced in session come from the same instructional systems shown in the curriculum architecture case study — PD participants work with real materials, not hypotheticals.

Learning principles applied

Andragogy / adult learning

Prior experience, problem-centered tasks, and immediate applicability.

ARCS

Attention and relevance framed around real instructional pain points.

Gradual Release

Model → guide → independent planning inside the session.

Transfer design

Session ends with a commitment and tool for the next performance context.

Outcome

The intended outcome is for participants to leave with practiced moves and a near-term application plan. As portfolio evidence, this work shows adult learning design capability developed through lead-teacher, mentor, and PD facilitation roles—applicable beyond K–12 settings.

Evidence available: session agenda and transfer job aid. Next evaluation: observe a first classroom run and use participant feedback to revise the session.

Reflection

Facilitating adults taught me that credibility comes from useful structure, not from talking longer. The same discipline shows up in eLearning and curriculum systems work: respect the learner’s time, design the practice, and make the next step obvious.
